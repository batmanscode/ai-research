A thought experiment / Research note

Who gets the
Infinity Stones?

Hand them to one person. Hope they destroy them. Now suppose the factories can make more.

We proved a small but surprising result: the average time until the stones return cannot, by itself, guarantee that a risky handover is worth it.

Try the three worlds ↓

The scenario belongs to CYBRDELIC (@cybrdelic) ↗
31 August 2026. The model and its assumptions are ours.

The risky partAll six stones

Call them Thanos, if you like.
The second handover matters too.

The rules we added

Destruction is a pause.
It may not be an ending.

01

Keep the stones

Everyone keeps their own stones. They can shield themselves from attacks, but we assume some danger remains.

02

Risk the handover

One person gets all the stones. They might use that power against everyone. If they destroy the stones instead, the world is safe for a while.

03

Meet the factories again

Factories eventually make more stones. You face the same choice again. Every handover is a fresh gamble: a past success does not make the next one safe.

What “better” means here: more safe time, on average. These are made-up risks for a thought experiment.

The proved result / Within this model

The average alone
cannot clear the handover.

If you give any extra weight to safety soon, an average pause alone cannot justify even a tiny handover risk. Some pattern with that same average will make keeping the stones better.

Make early returns very common. Offset them with rare, very long pauses. The average stays fixed while the tolerable handover risk approaches zero.

Knowing the stones cannot return for a certain time changes that. So does permanent removal of production. The theorem identifies the missing information; it does not say disarmament always fails.

The rule behind the bars

A handover is worth its risk when its chance of failure stays at or below this limit:

q ≤ λH1 + λH

q
Chance of failure at each handover.
λ
Danger rate while everyone keeps their stones.
H
Average safe time bought by one successful handover, using your time weights.

For the same background danger, more safe time between handovers means more handover risk is worth taking. At the limit, keeping and handing over tie.

How do the time weights enter?

Let R be a pause's length and β the rate chosen under “How much nearer years matter.” E just means “take the average over possible pauses.”

H = E[1 − e−βRβ]

This expression is for β > 0. At β = 0, every year counts equally: H = E[R], the average pause. Both cases use the rule above.

One more twist

Perfect patience changes
what the average can tell you.

Count a safe year far in the future just as much as a safe year today, and the average becomes enough. All three cards agree. Move “How much nearer years matter” to zero to see it.

Give even a little extra weight to nearer years, and some extremely uneven pattern can still spoil that guarantee. As you become more patient, that troublesome pattern becomes more extreme. No single world suddenly jumps from one answer to another.

Read before generalizing

The assumptions do real work.

Fresh risk, every time

This is not one immortal person's hidden good-or-evil personality. If a successful first handover proves that the person is permanently trustworthy, the comeback pattern stops mattering. The proof explains that alternative too.

We only count safety

We do not price freedom, inequality, useful applications, or the incentives of an elite. The note addresses a narrower question than the original three-way dilemma.

The rules stay the same

We assume each round starts fresh, with the same rules. Factories and handovers do not influence each other's luck. Learning, better technology, or people deliberately rebuilding weapons could change the answer.

What is new here?

This note pins down exactly what an average and time limits can tell us in this scenario. The underlying maths and the problem of trusting someone during disarmament are older ideas. We have not established that nobody has derived this result before. External peer review is still pending.
