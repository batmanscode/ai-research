A 4/3 lower bound
for secure domination.

Secure guards must answer attacks without leaving the network exposed. A certified 12-vertex counterexample proves that the best universal multiplier for this family of graphs is at least 4/3.

γs(G)= 4>α(G)= 3

γs is the fewest secure guards; α is the largest set of points with no links between them.

The problem

What does it mean to guard a network securely?

1

Cover

Every unguarded point must touch at least one guard. That is ordinary domination.

2

Respond

If any unguarded point is attacked, an adjacent guard moves there.

3

Stay covered

After the move, every point must still touch a guard. That extra condition is security.

How it was found

The failed ideas mattered.

  1. 01

    Small graphs agreed

    Every eligible graph with up to 10 vertices obeyed γs ≤ α, so the stronger statement looked plausible.

  2. 02

    False shortcuts broke

    Maximum independent sets, dominating paths, and a tempting dominating-edge lemma all failed as general proof routes.

  3. 03

    SAT searched what remained

    The search required α = 3, no induced P₅, connectivity, and failure of every triple—without enumerating every graph.

  4. 04

    Symmetry revealed the object

    The 12-vertex model was recognized as the complement of an icosahedron, then independently verified without the SAT clauses.

The status, precisely

The counterexample is proved. Two larger questions are not.

Proved

The 12-vertex witness

This exact graph has α = 3 and γs = 4. That is a theorem established by exhaustive verification, not a conjecture. The published 3α/2 theorem still holds: 4 ≤ 4.5.

Evidence

Smallest possible order

Searches exclude smaller examples, but this repository does not include a checkable SAT proof trace. Minimum order is therefore not claimed as a theorem here.

Open

The optimal coefficient

The exact answer remains somewhere between 4/3 and 3/2. The counterexample fixes the lower end, not the final value.

What remains open

The answer lives somewhere between 4/3 and 3/2.

The counterexample rules out coefficient 1; it does not contradict the published 3/2 theorem or show that bound is optimal. Finding an α = 4 graph with γs = 6 would settle optimality; targeted searches found no such graph through order 14.
